# Psychology & Persuasion Strategy — New United Rubber Cork

> **Companion document to `prompt.md`**  
> **Brand:** New United Rubber Cork  
> **Reference website:** https://amcorkindia.com/index.php  
> **Purpose:** Define the customer psychology, trust strategy, messaging principles and conversion experience that should guide every page of the redesigned website.

---

## 1. The Core Principle: Do Not Just Describe the Company. Make Buyers Feel Confident Choosing It.

A website should do more than explain what a business makes or sells. It should help the right visitor decide that this is a credible company worth contacting.

For New United Rubber Cork, the goal is not simply to display cork products, list industries or publish a company profile. The website must reduce buyer uncertainty and give visitors reasons to trust the company before the first conversation.

A buyer should leave the website thinking:

- “They understand the kind of product I need.”
- “They seem technically capable and professional.”
- “They can explain whether their product is suitable for my application.”
- “I understand what information to share to get a useful response.”
- “Contacting them feels like a sensible next step.”

**The website should sell confidence, not just products.**

This is especially important in B2B manufacturing, where a purchasing decision can affect product performance, production schedules, quality, cost and the buyer's reputation inside their own organisation.

## 2. Understand the B2B Buyer’s Psychology

A manufacturing buyer is rarely purchasing only a material or component. They are trying to solve a business or engineering problem while managing risk.

Different visitors may have different priorities:

| Visitor | What they may care about | What the website should help them understand |
|---|---|---|
| Procurement manager | Reliable supply, pricing, communication, specifications | How to enquire and what details are needed |
| Design / product engineer | Material suitability, dimensions, performance, tolerances | Available technical information and how to discuss an application |
| Manufacturer | Consistency, production requirements, repeat orders | Product range, capabilities and enquiry process |
| Distributor / reseller | Range, availability, commercial relationship | Which products are offered and how to contact the company |
| First-time buyer | Credibility, clarity, uncertainty | Who the company is, what it offers and what happens next |

Do not assume every visitor has the same knowledge. Some know the exact product and specification; others only know the problem they need to solve.

### Practical implication
Organise information around buyer questions and tasks, not only around the company's internal departments or history.

## 3. Positioning: Communicate a Point of View

A company becomes more memorable when it communicates how it thinks about its work, not just what it offers.

New United Rubber Cork should express a clear, credible point of view about product selection, material requirements, manufacturing and customer needs.

Potential positioning themes to validate with the company:

- Choosing the right material starts with understanding the application.
- Product suitability matters as much as product availability.
- Technical requirements should be discussed clearly before an order.
- Good manufacturing relationships depend on communication and consistency.
- The right solution is the one that fits the customer's real requirement.

These are strategic directions, not claims about existing company processes. Confirm that the company genuinely operates this way before presenting them as its philosophy.

### Example messaging direction

**Headline:** The right material starts with the right understanding.

**Supporting copy:** Every application has its own requirements. Tell us what you are working on, and our team can help you explore the relevant cork and rubberised-cork products offered by New United Rubber Cork.

Use this only if the company can genuinely provide that level of product guidance.

### Avoid generic positioning
Do not rely on statements such as:
- “We are the best.”
- “World-class quality.”
- “Your trusted partner.”
- “Leading manufacturer.”
- “Unmatched excellence.”

These phrases are easy for any competitor to copy and do not provide evidence.

## 4. Demonstrate Judgement, Not Just Capability

A list of machines, products, services or industries does not automatically prove that a company makes good decisions.

Buyers want confidence that the supplier understands requirements and communicates honestly.

The website should demonstrate judgement through useful explanations and real examples.

### Ways to demonstrate judgement

1. **Explain product selection.**  
   Where verified technical knowledge is available, explain which factors affect product selection, such as application, dimensions, operating conditions or material requirements.

2. **Explain when more information is needed.**  
   Show buyers that accurate recommendations depend on understanding their actual requirement. Do not pretend every application can be assessed from a product name alone.

3. **Explain trade-offs honestly.**  
   If the company can substantiate it, explain that a suitable solution depends on the application rather than suggesting one product is ideal for every situation.

4. **Show a real problem-solving example.**  
   Use a short, approved case study showing the customer's requirement, the challenge, the company's contribution and the verified outcome.

5. **Make technical information understandable.**  
   Present specifications in a clean table and explain unfamiliar terms when useful.

### Case-study structure

Use this format only when a genuine customer example is available and approved:

- **Requirement:** What did the customer need?
- **Challenge:** What made the requirement specific or difficult?
- **Assessment:** What factors were considered?
- **Approach:** What product or manufacturing approach was selected?
- **Outcome:** What happened, using only verified results?
- **Lesson:** What can another buyer learn from the example?

Never invent customer stories, quotes, outcomes, company names or performance figures. If no approved case study exists, do not fabricate one. Use a useful product-selection guide or a verified manufacturing explanation instead.

## 5. Let Visitors Experience What It Is Like to Work With the Company

Buyers are not only evaluating the product. They are also evaluating the experience of dealing with the supplier.

The website should make the next steps predictable and reassuring.

### Show a simple working process

A possible enquiry journey:

1. **Share your requirement**  
   The visitor provides the product of interest, application, dimensions, quantity and any available drawing or specification.

2. **Requirement review**  
   The company reviews the information and identifies any important missing details.

3. **Discussion and clarification**  
   The company contacts the buyer to clarify requirements where needed.

4. **Quotation / next steps**  
   The company responds with the relevant commercial or technical next step, subject to its actual process.

5. **Order coordination**  
   Explain what happens after confirmation only when the company's real process is known.

This is a suggested structure. Validate it with the company before publishing it as the actual workflow.

### Why this works
- It reduces uncertainty.
- It tells visitors what to prepare.
- It makes the company feel organised.
- It lowers the perceived effort of making contact.
- It helps attract more complete and useful enquiries.

Do not promise response times, delivery times, technical consultation or service levels unless the company can consistently honour them.

## 6. Build Trust Before Asking for the Enquiry

Trust should be built throughout the website, not left to one “Why Choose Us” section.

### Trust signals to prioritise

Use real, verifiable material such as:

- Genuine product photographs.
- Actual manufacturing or facility photographs.
- Accurate technical specifications.
- Clear company address and contact details.
- A coherent product catalogue.
- Real quality processes and certifications, if documented.
- Approved customer testimonials.
- Approved case studies.
- Clear enquiry steps.
- Consistent branding and professional presentation.
- Helpful responses to common buyer questions.

### Trust hierarchy

Prioritise evidence in this order:

1. **Direct evidence:** Real products, specifications, processes and facility images.
2. **Verifiable proof:** Certifications, documented capabilities, approved customer references and case studies.
3. **Clear explanations:** How products work, where they are used and what information is needed.
4. **Professional signals:** Consistent design, working links, accurate details and clear communication.
5. **Marketing statements:** Short claims supported by the evidence above.

Do not use fake reviews, invented logos, fabricated certifications, stock photos presented as company facilities, false counters or unsupported claims.

## 7. Make the Website Buyer-Centred, Not Company-Centred

A common company website mistake is to structure every page around what the business wants to say:

- Our company.
- Our mission.
- Our vision.
- Our values.
- Our achievements.
- Our products.

These may be relevant, but buyers usually arrive with a question or task.

### Use buyer-centred framing

| Company-centred wording | More buyer-centred direction |
|---|---|
| Our Products | Find the product for your requirement |
| Our Quality | Understand our quality approach |
| Our Capabilities | Explore what we can manufacture or supply |
| Contact Us | Discuss your product requirement |
| Our Experience | See the experience and evidence relevant to your application |
| Product Enquiry | Request information or a quotation for this product |

Do not force awkward marketing language into every heading. The aim is clarity, not cleverness.

### Page-level question
For every section, ask:

**“What question does this section answer for the buyer?”**

If a section does not answer a useful question, build trust, aid discovery or move the visitor towards a sensible next step, reconsider its purpose.

## 8. Reduce Cognitive Load

Visitors should not have to work hard to understand the website.

### Use progressive disclosure

Show the most important information first and provide deeper technical detail when the visitor needs it.

For example:

- Product card: product name, image and short explanation.
- Product page: overview, main specifications and applications.
- Technical section: detailed tables, documents or downloadable specifications where available.
- Enquiry form: collect the details needed for a useful response.

### Content rules
- One main message per section.
- Short paragraphs.
- Descriptive headings.
- Familiar language.
- Bullets for lists.
- Tables for technical specifications.
- Clear labels for technical units.
- Helpful explanations for specialist terms.
- Avoid repeating the same company introduction on every page.

### Do not remove necessary information
Minimal does not mean incomplete. Technical buyers may need detailed specifications to make a decision. Keep those details available and organise them well rather than hiding or deleting them.

## 9. Use Visuals to Create Understanding and Confidence

Visuals should do a job. They should not merely decorate the page.

### Prioritise
- Real product photography.
- Close-ups showing material texture and finish.
- Product dimensions or diagrams when accurate.
- Manufacturing photographs that represent the actual facility.
- Application images that genuinely relate to the product.
- Simple process diagrams.
- Technical tables and specification layouts.

### Visual hierarchy
- Give the product or material the strongest visual emphasis.
- Use concise copy beside relevant imagery.
- Keep backgrounds neutral and allow the product to stand out.
- Use consistent image ratios without making every section identical.
- Avoid irrelevant lifestyle photography.

### Image credibility
Never show a generic cork or factory image in a way that implies it is a photograph of New United Rubber Cork's actual product or facility. Label illustrative imagery appropriately where needed.

## 10. Conversion Psychology: Make the Next Step Feel Easy

A visitor may be interested but not ready to fill out a long form. Make it easy to take a small, relevant next step.

### Primary action
**Request a Quote**

### Supporting actions
- Explore Products.
- View Specifications.
- Ask About This Product.
- Discuss Your Requirement.
- Contact the Team.

### Product-specific enquiry
A quote CTA on a product page should retain the product name when the visitor moves to the form. This avoids making the buyer repeat information.

### Form design principles
- Ask only for information that is useful at this stage.
- Make required fields obvious.
- Explain why technical details are helpful.
- Allow the buyer to describe an unusual requirement.
- Offer a drawing/specification upload only if it is securely supported.
- Show clear validation and submission feedback.
- Do not claim an enquiry was sent unless the backend confirms success.

### Suggested form introduction
**Tell us what you need.**

Share the product, application or specification you have in mind. The more relevant detail you provide, the easier it is for the team to understand your enquiry.

Only promise a follow-up process that the company can actually deliver.

## 11. Pricing, Risk and Value

Do not assume that every buyer chooses the cheapest product or supplier. Buyers may also consider suitability, consistency, communication, quality evidence, supply reliability and the cost of getting the decision wrong.

The website should help visitors understand value without making unsupported price or performance claims.

### Messaging principles
- Explain product suitability where technical evidence supports it.
- Make available specifications easy to compare.
- Explain customisation only where offered.
- Be transparent about what needs discussion.
- Do not publish invented prices or savings.
- Do not claim a product reduces costs, failures or downtime without evidence.

Where prices are not published, make the quotation process clear and explain what information helps the company prepare a relevant quote.

## 12. Content Framework for Important Pages

### Homepage
The homepage should answer, in order:

1. What does New United Rubber Cork offer?
2. Which products can I explore?
3. What applications or industries are relevant?
4. What evidence makes the company credible?
5. What should I do next?

Do not turn the homepage into a complete company brochure.

### About page
Explain:
- What the company does.
- Its genuine background.
- Its approach to products and customers.
- Verified capabilities and evidence.
- What a buyer can expect when making an enquiry.

Use the company's real story, not generic mission/vision filler.

### Product page
Explain:
- What the product is.
- What it is used for.
- Which specifications are available.
- What information affects selection.
- What the buyer should share when enquiring.

### Contact / Quote page
Explain:
- How to reach the company.
- What information to provide.
- What happens after submitting an enquiry, based on the real process.

## 13. Tone of Voice

The brand should sound like a knowledgeable, straightforward industrial business.

### Use a tone that is
- Confident but not arrogant.
- Helpful but not overfamiliar.
- Technically informed but understandable.
- Clear and specific.
- Honest about limitations and unknowns.
- Professional for international B2B audiences.

### Example

Instead of:
“New United Rubber Cork is the number one company providing the best quality products with unmatched excellence.”

Prefer:
“Explore our cork and rubberised-cork products, review the available specifications and share your application requirements with our team.”

The second version is more useful because it tells the visitor what they can do and avoids unsupported claims.

## 14. Seven Questions to Ask Before Approving Any Section

Before implementing or approving a page section, ask:

1. **Relevance:** Does this matter to a real buyer?
2. **Clarity:** Can a visitor understand it quickly?
3. **Evidence:** Is every factual claim supported?
4. **Trust:** Does it make the company more credible?
5. **Differentiation:** Does it communicate something meaningful beyond generic industry language?
6. **Usability:** Does it help the visitor find information or take action?
7. **Conversion:** Does it make the next sensible step easier?

If a section fails most of these checks, simplify it, rewrite it or remove it.

**Do not design a website that only says, “Here is our company and here are our products.” Design a website that helps the right buyer think, “They understand what I need, they appear credible, and I feel comfortable starting a conversation with them.”**
